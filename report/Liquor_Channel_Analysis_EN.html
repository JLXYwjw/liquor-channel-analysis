Liquor Channel Reconciliation & Sales Performance

Analysis of 308k liquor wholesale sales (2017–2020): reconcile the retail/warehouse/transfer channel structure, quantify channel coverage gaps and supplier concentration, and identify high-performing categories & assortment directions.

Data: liquor wholesale sales · 307,645 lines / 37,321 items / 397 suppliers · 2017–2020
1 · Objective 2 · Cleaning 3 · Metrics & Charts 4 · Performance Modeling 5 · Recommendations
Part 1 · Business Objective

1. Why this analysis

A liquor wholesaler ships through three channels: warehouse wholesale (to downstream retailers), retail (direct/store), transfer (internal inter-warehouse). Core questions:

  • Channel reconciliation: what is the volume structure across channels? Which items have retail = 0 (channel coverage gap)? Is transfer reasonable?
  • Category & supplier performance: which of WINE/BEER/LIQUOR is growing? Is the supplier base over-concentrated (pricing & supply-cut risk)?

One-line goal: use channel reconciliation to see "which channel the goods go through and who contributes volume", identify coverage gaps & concentration risk, and guide channel & assortment decisions.

Honest boundary. Monthly data is non-continuous (2018 only Jan–Feb, 2020 has missing stretches, 24 valid months total) → monthly forecasting is unreliable (Part 4, R² negative); some categories contain negative values (returns/adjustments, e.g. OTHER -142k) — identified but not used as the main analysis. Reconciliation & analysis focus on totals and structure, not unreliable time-series extrapolation.

Insight

Warehouse wholesale is the absolute main channel (64.4%), retail only 17.9%. Nearly 40% of lines have retail = 0 (volume via wholesale/transfer only) — the book is mainly a B2B wholesale structure, with limited retail direct coverage.

Action

For high-volume but retail=0 items, assess adding a retail channel (direct margin is usually higher); if retail isn't a target channel, clearly stay wholesale-first and cut needless transfer movement.

Part 4 · Performance Modeling

4. Supplier performance clustering (principle · why · how)

Why clustering. The 397 suppliers differ widely in scale, channel and item count; unsupervised clustering groups them into similar-performance groups, then procurement/management resources are allocated accordingly — implementable without labels.

Principle & how. Standardize supplier volume (log), wholesale share, retail share, item count (log), then KMeans into 4 groups:

GroupCountAvg volumeProfile
C0 large wholesale93109kHigh volume, retail 15%, many items (~335) — core wholesale suppliers
C3 medium balanced10817.9kMedium volume, retail 40%, ~41 items — potential suppliers
C2 small warehouse111653Low volume, retail 8% — long-tail wholesale
C1 retail small85-7k*Retail share 64% but small volume (includes negative returns) — retail small traders

*C1 avg volume includes negative-value suppliers (returns/adjustments), not true core business; profile only.

Honest: no monthly forecast. Data has only 24 valid months (2018/2020 largely missing); category monthly forecast has mean linear R² = -1.89 (worse than using the mean) — samples non-continuous, series too short, forecast unreliable. So this project does not use forecasting as a recommendation basis; it provides a current-state diagnosis of structure & performance. Consistent with "state honestly when data is insufficient, no fake predictions".

Why not deep learning. Tabular, very few monthly samples (<30 points), no learnable sequence; clustering & statistics are explainable and directly implementable. DL lacks data and is unexplainable here — consistent with "use only when the business needs it".
